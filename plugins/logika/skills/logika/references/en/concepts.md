# Concepts: classification, definition, and division

## Classes of concepts

| Distinction | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Individual / general | One object / a class of similar objects | Newton / scientist; Mount Kazbek / mountain |
| Collective / distributive | A group as a whole / its members individually | A regiment crossed a river / every soldier was tired |
| Concrete / abstract | A thing or quality-bearing object / a quality considered separately | A white stone / whiteness |
| Positive / negative | Presence / absence of an attribute | Finite / infinite; careful / careless |
| Relative / absolute | A concept presupposes a correlate / does not do so | Parent presupposes child; cause presupposes effect / stone |

In the textbook's terminology, an adjective describing a thing is concrete,
whereas a noun such as “whiteness” names an abstract quality. Classify the use of
the term in context: “regiment” can name a class of regiments or a collective unit.
Do not infer that a statement about a whole holds of every member.

## Intension and extension

**Intension** is the set of attributes contained in a concept. **Extension** is
the set of objects to which it applies. Adding a restricting attribute narrows
the extension; removing it broadens the extension. For example, “tree,” “palm,”
and “coconut palm” successively add restrictions.

- **Genus:** a wider concept containing a species.
- **Species:** a narrower concept within a genus; it can itself be a genus for
  further subdivisions.
- **Summum genus:** a highest genus in the classification.
- **Infima species:** a lowest species, divided further only into individuals.
- **Genus proximum:** the nearest genus of the concept being defined.
- **Restriction** (*determinatio*): add attributes to make a less general concept.
- **Generalization** (*generalisatio*): remove attributes to make a more general one.

The inverse relation concerns adding or removing genuine restrictions. Adding
an attribute already entailed by the existing definition need not change the
extension.

## Five predicables

1. **Genus:** the class under which a concept falls; chemistry is a science.
2. **Specific difference** (*differentia specifica*): the feature distinguishing
   the species from others in the genus.
3. **Species:** genus plus specific difference; an arsenal is a building used to
   store weapons.
4. **Property** (*proprium*): a characteristic following from the essential
   attributes but not itself part of the definition. In Euclidean geometry,
   having angles summing to two right angles is a property of a triangle.
5. **Accident** (*accidens*): a feature not entailed by the essential attributes.
   Traditional classifications distinguish separable and inseparable accidents.
   A feature that happens to accompany observed cases is not automatically essential.

## Relations between concepts

- **Subordination** (*subordinatio*): a species falls under a genus, such as birch
  under tree.
- **Coordination** (*coordinatio*): several species fall under one genus.
- **Coextension** (*aequipollentia*): different descriptions pick out the same
  objects, such as squares and equal-sided rectangles in Euclidean geometry.
- **Contrariety** (*contraria*): opposed determinate alternatives within a genus,
  such as white and black among colors. They need not exhaust the genus.
- **Contradiction** (*contradictoria*): A and non-A exhaust and exclude each other
  within the specified domain. Non-white does not specify a particular other color.
- **Intersection:** extensions overlap without either containing the other;
  writers and scientists are an example.
- **Incomparability** (*disparata*): no relevant common genus or basis of comparison
  has been identified. Merely calling two things “objects” may not make the proposed
  comparison informative.

## Definition

Define through the nearest genus and specific difference:
*definitio fit per genus proximum et differentiam specificam*.
The **definiendum** is what is defined; the **definiens** supplies the definition.

Example: a rectangle is a parallelogram whose angles are all right angles.
Simple qualities and individual objects may require another explanatory method
rather than a genus-and-differentia definition.

### Rules

1. **Coextensiveness:** the definition must cover exactly the intended objects.
   “A horse is a domestic animal” is too broad. Defining every triangle as having
   three equal sides is too narrow.
2. **No circularity:** do not define the explanatory term through the term being
   explained. “Light is that which possesses light” is *idem per idem*.
3. **State what the thing is:** a merely negative definition usually leaves too
   many possibilities. “A theater is a building not used as a dwelling” is too
   broad. Negative concepts can require a negative definition, such as a
   nonresident defined by the relevant residence criterion.
4. **Clarity:** avoid metaphor, ambiguity, and explaining the unknown by something
   even less known (*ignotum per ignotius*). “Architecture is frozen music” is a
   metaphor, not a logical definition.

### Alternatives to definition

- **Ostension:** point to a perceptible example, such as a color or sound.
- **Description:** enumerate attributes of an individual object.
- **Characterization:** select its distinguishing or prominent traits.
- **Comparison:** explain through a relevant resemblance to something familiar.
- **Distinction:** explain how it differs from a similar concept.

## Division

Definition explains intension; division reveals extension by enumerating species.

- **Whole to be divided** (*totum dividendum*): the concept under examination.
- **Members of division** (*membra divisionis*): the resulting species.
- **Basis of division** (*fundamentum divisionis*): the attribute used to separate them.

Triangles divided by their angles are acute, right, or obtuse. Classification by
side lengths uses a different basis. State whether “isosceles” includes equilateral
triangles; if it does, those two labels are not mutually exclusive members.
At a later subdivision, a new basis can be introduced explicitly.

### Dichotomy

Divide A into B and non-B. This is exhaustive within A, but the negative member
remains unspecified. For example, trees can be divided into conifers and
non-conifers, with further divisions of the latter group.

### Rules

1. **Exhaustiveness and proper scope:** members must jointly cover the divided
   concept and contain nothing outside it. Acute and obtuse omit right triangles;
   including violets among tree species exceeds the scope.
2. **Mutual exclusion:** members must not overlap. French books, German books,
   and dictionaries fail this rule.
3. **One basis at a time:** do not mix language, nationality, religion, or another
   criterion at the same level without explicitly defining a cross-classification.
4. **Continuity:** when presenting a hierarchy, move through its stated immediate
   subdivisions rather than mixing levels. Judge this against the hierarchy in use,
   not an assumed universal taxonomy.

## Worked checks

“A circle is a figure without corners” is too broad and gives only a negative
condition: an ellipse also qualifies.

“Triangles are equilateral, isosceles, or right-angled” mixes side and angle
criteria; a right triangle can also be isosceles.

“The regiment crossed the river” is a collective use. It does not establish,
without additional information, that every individual soldier crossed.
