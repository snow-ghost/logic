# Logical errors, sophisms, and paralogisms

Separate an invalid inference, a false premise, an unsupported premise, and a
weak non-deductive inference. They require different explanations and repairs.
Give a conventional Latin name when available; do not invent one for every issue.

## Errors of expression

**Equivocation / homonymy** (*equivocatio*, *homonymia*): one expression is used
with different meanings. In a debate about free speech, a legal right and freedom
from any social consequences are distinct concepts. State the shift instead of
treating the two claims as if they concerned the same thing.

## Errors concerning the thesis

### Irrelevant conclusion (*ignoratio elenchi*)

The argument establishes something other than the thesis. Responding to a
privacy concern by citing an application's download count establishes popularity,
not privacy. Showing that others escaped punishment does not prove innocence.

### Proving too little or too much

An argument proves **too little** when it supports only part of the thesis.
“No misconduct is known” is not enough to establish every claimed virtue.

It proves **too much** when the proposed principle also entails conclusions
that undermine that principle: *qui nimium probat, nihil probat*. Identify the
actual unwanted implication and why it matters; merely suggesting an analogy
is not a refutation.

### Ad hominem

Attacking a speaker's personal traits replaces evaluation of the argument.
The author's disliked affiliation does not by itself refute a scientific claim.
Credentials, reliability, or conflicts of interest can be relevant to testimony;
explain when a personal consideration is being used irrelevantly.

## Errors concerning premises

### False foundation (*error fundamentalis*)

A false basic proposition supports the rest of a proof. Establish its falsity
with appropriate evidence; absence of evidence is not enough to call it false.

### Begging the question (*petitio principii*)

A premise already assumes the disputed conclusion. Distinguish this circular
dependence from a premise that is merely unstated or insufficiently supported.

### Restatement as explanation (*idem per idem*)

The claim is repeated in different words. “We can see through glass because it
is transparent” names the property but, in a request for a causal mechanism,
does not explain how it occurs.

### Circular proof (*circulus in demonstrando*)

A is supported by B and B by A. “The report is trustworthy because its author is
honest; the author's honesty is established by the report” supplies no independent ground.

### Dropping qualifications

*A dicto secundum quid ad dictum simpliciter*: a claim true under specified
conditions is treated as unconditional. A study of a particular dose, population,
or workload cannot be reported as a universal conclusion without justification.

### Division

*A sensu composito ad sensum divisum*: attribute a property of the whole to every
part. An organization adopting a decision does not establish that every member
supported it.

### Composition

*A sensu diviso ad sensum compositum*: transfer a property of each part to the
whole. Each expense being affordable does not establish that their total is
affordable. Whether a transfer is valid depends on the property in question.

## Errors in inference form

- **Undistributed middle:** the middle term is distributed in neither premise.
- **Illicit major/minor** (*illicitus processus*): a term is distributed in the
  conclusion but not in its premise.
- **Four terms** (*quaternio terminorum*): an apparent shared term changes meaning.
- **Affirming the consequent:** if A then B; B; therefore A.
- **Denying the antecedent:** if A then B; not-A; therefore not-B.
- **False dilemma:** the proposed alternatives omit relevant possibilities.
- **Existential fallacy:** infer existence from universal premises without the
  required existence assumption in a framework that permits empty classes.

Use [the syllogism rules](syllogism.md) and [judgment forms](judgments.md) to show
the particular violation rather than only attaching a label.

## Errors in induction and analogy

### Hasty generalization (*fallacia fictae universalitatis*)

Generalize from inadequate or unrepresentative observations. Two rude comments
do not establish a trait of a city's entire population. Check sample selection,
size, variation, and relevant counterexamples.

### Post hoc / false cause

*Post hoc ergo propter hoc* treats “after” as “because of.” *Non causa pro causa*
mistakes something that is not the cause for the cause. Sales falling after a
software change calls for investigation; the temporal order alone is insufficient.

### One-sided enumeration

Count confirming instances while overlooking contrary ones. Recalling successful
predictions but forgetting failed predictions biases the result. “It has happened
so far” also needs support before becoming “It will always happen.”

### False analogy

Transfer an unobserved property based on similarities unrelated to that property.
Assess relevant common features and differences. A state and a household having
budgets does not make all financial constraints identical.

## Sophisms and paralogisms

A **paralogism** is an unintentional error; a **sophism** is deliberately misleading
reasoning. An error alone does not establish the speaker's intention.

Traditional examples:

1. **The liar:** a habitual liar makes one truthful admission. Treating a true
   statement as proof that the speaker is never a liar confuses a single act with
   a character description. This example is distinct from the self-referential
   semantic liar paradox.
2. **The horned person:** “You have what you have not lost; you have not lost horns;
   therefore you have horns.” The first premise needs a prior-possession condition.
3. **The heap:** removing one grain is repeatedly assumed never to change a heap
   into a non-heap. Examine the vague predicate and the unrestricted transition
   assumption; the absence of a convenient boundary does not settle every case.
4. **Euathlus and Protagoras:** a fee is due after the student's first successful
   lawsuit; teacher and student each combine the expected judgment with the
   contractual condition to claim a favorable result. Separate the questions of
   the contractual trigger, the judgment, and when each takes effect.

## Reporting an issue

Quote the relevant passage, reconstruct its inference, identify the missing or
invalid step, and suggest the smallest defensible repair. If the evidence could
support a qualified claim, state the qualification. If it cannot, mark the need
for evidence rather than supplying invented observations.
